### batra-2005 — the round-3 flag is CONFIRMED. This is PREVENTION, not rescue.

This changes what the card should say, so it is stated plainly.

- Title, verbatim: "The efficacy of a subhypnotic dose of propofol in **preventing**
  laryngospasm following tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy in children"
- PubMed publication type: Randomized Controlled Trial. MeSH term:
  **Laryngismus / prevention & control**.
- Drug timing, verbatim: "Before extubation, the patients were randomized and received in
  a blinded fashion either propofol 0.5 mg.kg(-1) or saline (control) intravenously.
  Tracheal extubation was performed 60 s after administration of study drug".
- Result, verbatim: "Laryngospasm was seen in 20% (n = 12) of the 60 children in the
  control group and in only 6.6% (n = 4) of 60 children in the propofol group (P < 0.05)."
- Conclusion, verbatim: "During emergence from inhalational anesthesia, propofol in a
  subhypnotic dose (0.5 mg.kg(-1)) **decreases the likelihood** of laryngospasm upon
  tracheal extubation in children".

**The card teaches this as rescue. Batra 2005 does not support that.** The drug was given
before extubation to patients who had not developed laryngospasm. Qualifiers: children
aged 3 to 14, ASA I to II, tonsillectomy with or without adenoidectomy, single setting.

Note for completeness: a *separate* small literature does address propofol for rescue —
Visvanathan's reference list cites Afshan et al, "Is there a role of a small dose of
propofol in the treatment of laryngeal spasm?", Paediatr Anaesth 2002;12(7):625-628. **I
did not retrieve or read it.** If the card wants a rescue citation, that is the target,

### The Larson letter and the "unproven" rebuttal — BOTH retrieved

**larson-1998**, full text read under entitlement.

Notch location, verbatim: "This notch is behind the lobule of the pinna of each ear. It is
bounded anteriorly by the ascending ramus of the mandible adjacent to the condyle,
posteriorly by the mastoid process of the temporal bone, and cephalad by the base of the

Technique, verbatim: "The therapist presses very firmly inward toward the base of the
skull with both fingers, while at the same time lifting the mandible at a right angle to
the plane of the body (i.e., forward displacement of the mandible or 'jaw thrust').
Properly performed, it will convert laryngospasm within one or two breaths to laryngeal
stridor and in another few breaths to unobstructed respirations."

**The evidence base, in the author's own words.** "Almost 40 years ago, Dr. N. P. Guadagni
showed me a technique for prompt termination of laryngospasm, which I have used countless
times with complete success." And: "a thorough literature search has not revealed any
mention of it." And, on mechanism: "The obvious question is, Why does it work?
Unfortunately, a sound, scientific answer cannot be provided."

**There are no cases and no data in this letter.** It is personal practice. The card
should not imply otherwise.

**Citation trap.** The LWW byline renders the author as "Philip C. Larson". The signature
block reads "C. Philip Larson, Jr., M.D." LWW has the given and family names transposed.
Cite as Larson CP Jr.

**abelson-2015**, the later letter calling it unproven, full text read.

Verbatim: "Although LNP is an accepted technique advocated by eminent anesthesiologists,
there is insufficient published data to systematically evaluate its efficacy. Furthermore,
the line is blurred between LNP (which incorporates a special jaw thrust) and simple jaw
thrust (which may inadvertently overlap LNP)."

Abelson is not purely negative: he reports a single infant case (8-month-old, 6.7 kg, born
at 28 weeks) in which the manoeuvre broke laryngospasm **under continuous fiberoptic view
of the larynx**, which removes the usual diagnostic subjectivity. He calls for "A
controlled study of LNP that involves fiberoptic evaluation".

### Jaw thrust and CPAP — partial primary support, from an unexpected place

There is no trial of jaw thrust or CPAP for laryngospasm that I found. But two retrieved
sources place them explicitly in sequence:

- **abelson-2015**, verbatim: "First, one has optimized the upper airway with routine
  maneuvers (e.g., CPAP, chin lift, jaw thrust) to exclude supraglottic obstruction.
  Second, there is sufficient cardiopulmonary reserve to allow time to rescue the patient

  course."
- **larson-1998** opens by summarising what the textbooks said in 1998, verbatim:
  "standard textbooks of anesthesiology suggest virtually the same sequence: namely jaw

**Both are correspondence, not evidence.** Larson is explicitly characterising textbooks,
not reporting data. Use them as attributed statements about recommended practice, and keep
the round-3 finding that no society guideline states a management sequence.

Provenance note on a claim the card may inherit: Abelson's assertion that "Paralysis
definitively treats laryngospasm, or otherwise rules it out, in 100% of cases" is cited to
**weiss-engelhardt-2012**, which PubMed classifies as an **Editorial** with no abstract.
That "100%" traces to an editorial, not to a study.

Term counts across the entire full text: **anaphylaxis 0, sepsis 0, vasodilat\* 0,

**Therefore, explicitly NOT FOUND:** a sourced differential for postoperative hypotension
covering hypovolaemia and bleeding, vasodilatation from residual anaesthetic or neuraxial
sympathectomy, anaphylaxis, sepsis, and cardiac causes; and **NOT FOUND:** any sourced
basis for escalation to norepinephrine in the PACU.

**What POQI-3 does support, verbatim, and it is better than nothing:**

- "Practice recommendation 4: A structured bedside assessment should be carried out in
  response to postoperative hypotension/hypertension in order to (a) determine aetiology,
  (b) select appropriate treatment if indicated, and (c) consider changing the intensity of
  future monitoring and care environment."
- "Hypotension should be treated immediately in the symptomatic patient. For a positive PLR
  test, intravenous fluid would be appropriate in many instances. If preload augmentation
  is not needed, vasopressor or inotropic support is indicated."
- "Current evidence suggests that much postoperative hypotension is not caused by
  hypovolaemia, but treatment with a vasopressor or inotrope typically requires a
  high-intensity, monitored setting that has significant resource implications."
- From a cited prospective observational study in the recovery room, in spontaneously
  breathing postsurgical patients with suspected hypovolaemia (systolic <90 mm Hg, MAP
  <70 mm Hg, oliguria, or heart rate >100 beats/min): "only 54% responded to the fluid
  bolus. This suggests that the typical approach of correcting postoperative hypotension
  with intravenous fluid (preload correction) may be inappropriate ~50% of the time, with
  correction of vascular tone or inotropy being required in the remaining patients."
- From a cited meta-analysis of 2260 patients: "the mean prevalence of fluid responsiveness
  was 50%, and typical signs and symptoms of suspected hypovolaemia were not predictive of
  fluid responsiveness. However, an increase in cardiac output after passive leg raise
  (PLR) strongly predicted fluid responsiveness (positive likelihood ratio [LR]=11; 95%
  confidence interval [CI], 7.6-17; pooled specificity, 92%)." Negative LR 0.13 (95% CI
  0.07-0.22; pooled sensitivity 88%).

Scope qualifier, verbatim: the target population is "adult, non-cardiac surgical patients
in the postoperative phase outside of the ICU." Modified Delphi consensus, not evidence.

**Recommendation.** Rewrite the card from a cause list to a sourced approach: structured
bedside assessment to determine aetiology, passive leg raise to decide fluid versus
vasopressor or inotrope, and an explicit statement that much postoperative hypotension is
not hypovolaemic. Then carry a stated negative finding that no source was found for the
six-cause differential or for norepinephrine escalation. That is honest and it is better
teaching than the current card, which POQI-3 actively contradicts on reflexive fluids.

## 4. Welch 2009, for the Day 19 nerve injury rate

Retrieved. Anesthesiology 2009;111(3):490-497.

Verbatim: "Of all patients undergoing 380,680 anesthetics during a 10-yr period, 185
patients were initially identified as having nerve injuries, and after review, 112 met our
definition of a perioperative nerve injury (frequency = 0.03%)."

Associations found: hypertension, tobacco use, diabetes mellitus; general and epidural
anaesthesia; neurosurgery, cardiac, general and orthopaedic surgery.

**Qualifiers, which Day 19 should carry.** Injury was defined as "a new sensory and/or
motor deficit". Nerve complications from the surgical procedure itself were **excluded**.
Case-finding was retrospective, from quality assurance, closed claims and institution-wide
billing-code databases at a **single tertiary care university hospital**. This is a
detected and reported frequency, not a prospectively screened incidence, and is therefore
best presented as a **lower bound**. Day 19 can now replace "only a cardiac surgery series
of 3 in 575" with a general figure, but should say 0.03% *detected* rather than "the rate".

### hausmann-1991 — DO NOT RETIRE THE ROW. It is rescued.

PMID **2058825**, read on the PubMed record page. Anaesthesist 1991 Apr;40(4):229-234.

The oxygen-consumption figures, verbatim from the abstract:

- "Steady-state general anesthesia led to approximately 30% reduction in VO2 (K1: 121-225
