## 1. Days where the proposed evidence mode does not survive retrieval

**Day 1 — proposed trial_based. Does not survive as written.**
There is no randomised trial showing that residual block below TOF 0.9 causes PACU respiratory
morbidity, and there could not easily be one. Murphy 2008 is a matched case-control study
nested in a single-centre cohort. RECITE-US is a blinded observational cohort. POPULAR is a
22,803-patient prospective observational cohort. The 2023 ASA guideline is a guideline.
The claim itself is three claims with different evidentiary status:
- *common* — cohort evidence, strong and consistent (RECITE-US 64.7%).
- *undetectable on clinical exam* — cohort evidence, strong (RECITE-US patients were judged
  extubatable by clinical judgement and qualitative nerve stimulation).
- *most preventable cause of PACU respiratory morbidity* — this is the one with no trial behind
  it, and POPULAR actively complicates it by reporting that neither reversal agents nor
  neuromuscular monitoring reduced pulmonary complications in that cohort.
The day should be retagged consensus_only with the vocabulary term **registry** plus
**guideline**, or the claim narrowed. See section 2 for suggested wording.

**Day 4 — proposed trial_based. Does not survive.**
PRODIGY is prospective observational with blinded monitoring, not a trial. Taenzer 2010 is a
before-and-after study with concurrent controls. Sun 2015 is a blinded observational study.
Epstein 2014 is a retrospective analysis of recorded data. There is no randomised trial showing
that continuous monitoring changes outcome in the PACU. Retag consensus_only, resting on
**registry** and **physiology**.
Note also that the strongest temporal data (Driver, median time to first respiratory depression
event 8.8 hours) describes the ward, not the PACU. If the day is about the PACU specifically, its
best evidence is Epstein 2014, which is about the timing of desaturation within the PACU stay,
not about opioid-induced respiratory depression per se.

**Day 5 — proposed trial_based. Survives only in a qualified sense.**
Sessler 2018 is a substudy of a randomised trial, but hypotension was an observed exposure, not
a randomised allocation, so the design is a cohort analysis inside an RCT population. Liem 2020,
Salmasi 2017 and the POQI statements are cohort and consensus. The dose-and-duration structure
is real and quantified, but it is associational. Suggest tagging **trial_based (exposure
analysis within RCT cohorts)** if the taxonomy allows a qualifier, otherwise consensus_only on
**registry**. This distinction matters here more than elsewhere, because the day's whole point
is to move residents from observing a number to treating an exposure, and the evidence does not
establish that treating it changes anything.

**Day 8 — proposed trial_based. Does not survive.**
VISION is a prospective cohort. Botto 2014 is a cohort. The AHA statement is consensus. The claim
as written ("mostly silent, and the decision not to look is a decision") is fully supported by
cohort data. What has no trial behind it is the implied next step: that finding MINS and acting
on it improves anything. Retag consensus_only on **registry** plus **guideline**, and note in
the day itself that the screening-changes-outcome question is open.

**Day 12 — proposed trial_based. Partially survives, but not for the part the day emphasises.**
The 2024 ESAIC guideline rests on a large randomised literature for *prevention*. It does not
rest on trials for the entity-distinction claim (emergence agitation versus postoperative
delirium as different entities with different time courses) or for the claim that the hypoactive
form is both commoner and more often missed. Those are descriptive-epidemiology and
screening-performance claims. The guideline itself states that certainty of evidence across its
13 recommendations ranges from very low to moderate. Split the day, or retag consensus_only on
**guideline** with the prevention material flagged as the trial-based portion.

**Day 15 — proposed trial_based. Does not survive at all.**
This is the sharpest mismatch in the set. The claim is that mandatory oral intake and mandatory
voiding are institutional habit rather than criteria. The source that establishes this is the
2013 ASA postanesthetic care guideline, which reaches that position by declaring the literature
insufficient and then reporting an opinion survey. That is the opposite of trial evidence; it is
an absence of evidence plus a poll. Retag consensus_only, vocabulary term **practice_advisory**,
and see section 4 for the exact wording.

**Day 16 — proposed trial_based. Half survives.**
The handover half has two randomised checklist trials (Salzwedel 2013, Salzwedel 2016), neither
of which I could verify beyond a citation string, and one strong before-and-after cohort
(HATRICC) which found improved information transfer and **no change in ICU mortality or length of
stay**. The PACU-capacity-is-an-OR-problem half has no source in this ledger at all. See
section 5.

**Days 2, 3, 6, 10, 11, 13, 14 — proposed consensus_only. All survive.** Day 3's designation is
correct but for a reason worth stating: it is consensus_only resting almost entirely on
**physiology** (the alveolar gas equation) plus a single small human study, and the ledger has
one primary source for the whole day.

## 2. Days where the strongest available support is weaker than the claim

Suggested reframings are of the *claim*, not of teaching prose.

**Day 1.** Current: "...and the most preventable cause of PACU respiratory morbidity."
The superlative is not supported, and POPULAR points the other way on whether the available
preventive measures work. Suggested: *"Residual neuromuscular blockade below a TOF ratio of 0.9
is common, is not detectable by clinical examination or by qualitative nerve stimulation, and is
strongly associated with critical respiratory events in the PACU."* That version is fully
carried by Murphy 2008 and RECITE-US and survives POPULAR.

**Day 4.** Current: "...intermittent spot checks are structurally blind to it."
Supported for the general care floor. Not established for the PACU, where observation is
continuous by staffing rather than by device. Suggested: *"Opioid-induced respiratory depression
is episodic and mostly unwitnessed; on the general care floor, intermittent spot checks miss most
of it, and hypoxaemia in the PACU is not concentrated in the first thirty minutes where
observation is densest."* The second clause is Epstein 2014 and is PACU-specific.

**Day 7.** Current: "Perioperative hypothermia has outcome evidence most PACU interventions lack."
True in 1996 and now materially qualified. PROTECT (2022, n=5013, randomised) found no difference
in a 30-day composite of myocardial injury, cardiac arrest and death between core temperature
targets of 37 C and 35.5 C (9.9% versus 9.6%), and no difference in surgical site infection,
transfusion, length of stay or readmission. Suggested: *"Perioperative hypothermia has randomised
outcome evidence that most PACU interventions lack, but that evidence is about avoiding moderate
hypothermia, not about achieving 37 C; below roughly 35.5 C the old trials apply, and above it
PROTECT found nothing to gain."* This is the largest substantive change in the sixteen days.

**Day 8.** Current: "...and the decision not to look is a decision."
Supported as a statement about detection. Not supported as a statement about benefit. Suggested:
*"Myocardial injury after noncardiac surgery is mostly silent — only 15.8% of MINS patients in
VISION had an ischaemic symptom — and carries an adjusted 30-day mortality hazard of 3.87; the
decision not to look is therefore a decision about what you are willing not to know, though
whether looking changes outcome is unresolved."*

**Day 11.** Current: "...the block also masks compartment syndrome."
The strongest society statement I retrieved says the opposite of what this implies: the
ESRA/ASRA joint committee advisory states there are no current evidence-based data that regional
anaesthesia increases the risk of acute compartment syndrome or delays its diagnosis. That
advisory is paediatric in scope, which weakens it as a source for an adult curriculum, but it is
what exists. Suggested: *"Whether a block masks compartment syndrome is contested: society
statements find no evidence that regional anaesthesia delays diagnosis, while the case literature
and medicolegal practice treat breakthrough pain and rising analgesic requirement as the signal
to unblind the limb."* Per your instruction I am not resolving this; both positions are named
with their sources.

**Day 16.** Current: "...handover is a measurable risk point."
Measurable, yes — HATRICC measured information omissions against 13 defined topics. Risk point,
not demonstrated: HATRICC found ICU mortality and length of stay unchanged, and handover duration
roughly doubled. Suggested: *"Handover is a measurable process — information omissions can be
counted against a defined list — and standardisation reliably improves transfer of information,
though no study has yet shown it changes mortality or length of stay."*

## 3. Days where the literature has moved since the claim was drafted

**Day 9. The guideline has been replaced.** The Fifth Consensus Guidelines for the Management of
PONV appeared online ahead of print on 14 November 2025 and are now in print: Anesth Analg
2026;143(3):497-513, DOI 10.1213/ANE.0000000000007816, PMID 41237407. The Fourth (Gan 2020) is
superseded. Any page, pearl or quiz item written against the 2020 document needs re-checking
against the 2026 one — particularly the rescue-treatment rule, which is half of the day's claim.
New graded statements visible in the Fifth include A1 evidence that low-dose gabapentinoids
reduce PONV risk despite no significant analgesic effect, and A1 that opioid-free anaesthesia
reduces PONV versus opioid-based anaesthesia.

**Day 7. PROTECT (2022) postdates the framing.** Detailed above. Days 5 and 8 also touch this
trial's composite endpoint and should be checked for consistency with it.

**Day 12. The guideline has been replaced.** ESAIC 2024 (Eur J Anaesthesiol 2024;41(2):81-108)
supersedes ESAIC 2017. The 2024 update matters for the PACU specifically: it requires POD to be
measured with a validated screening tool at least once daily for at least three days, *starting
in the recovery room or PACU on the day of surgery, or at latest on postoperative day 1*. That
sentence puts delirium screening inside the PACU and is worth the day building around.

**Day 4. HATRICC-US and related work may have reported.** Also relevant to Day 16.

**Day 16. HATRICC-US (NCT04571749) may have published.** The multicentre stepped-wedge
type 2 hybrid effectiveness-implementation trial registered on 1 October 2020 was protocol-only
as of my retrieval. A five-year-old stepped-wedge trial very plausibly has results by now, and
they would be the strongest evidence the day could have. **Run this search before writing Day 16.**

## 4. Days 14 and 15: the guideline language verbatim

These two days are built on the weakness of the recommendation rather than its content, so the
exact wording and the exact strength qualifier are the material.

### Day 14 — ASA 2014 OSA guideline (Anesthesiology 2014;120(2):268-286)

The evidentiary statement, verbatim:

> "The literature is insufficient to offer guidance regarding the appropriate time for discharge"

— continuing, in the guideline's words, to say that this applies to patients at increased
perioperative risk from OSA being discharged from the surgical facility.

The recommendation that follows it does not carry an evidence grade. It carries an **opinion**
qualifier: the consultants and ASA members **strongly agree** that such patients should not be
discharged from the recovery area to an unmonitored setting — home or an unmonitored hospital bed
— until they are no longer at risk of postoperative respiratory depression.

Two things to teach from that pair. First, the strength language attaches to *agreement among
consultants*, not to evidence; "strongly agree" is a survey result. Second, the recommendation is
circular as an operational rule: it says do not discharge until the patient is no longer at risk,
while the preceding sentence says the literature cannot tell you when that is. The obligation is
real and the timing is undefined, and residents should be taught that shape rather than a number.

The 2016 SASM guideline hedges in a different register, stating that few randomised studies exist
in this field and that most recommendations were developed by experts through consensus
processes with evidence grading. Useful as contrast.

### Day 15 — ASA 2013 Postanesthetic Care guideline (Anesthesiology 2013;118(2):291-307)

Section V, Protocol for Discharge. The evidentiary statement on voiding, verbatim:

> "The literature is insufficient to evaluate the benefits of requiring"

— patients to urinate before discharge.

What sits beside it is not an evidence grade but an opinion survey, and the survey went the other
way from the convention:
- support for **not** requiring voiding before discharge: **85%**
- support for requiring a responsible individual to accompany the patient home: **95%**
- support for **not** requiring a mandatory minimum stay in recovery: **85%**

Requiring that patients drink water before discharge appears among the interventions ASA members
did **not** support.

On urine output more generally, the guideline states that the original literature indicated
assessment of urine output is effective in identifying patients with urinary retention (Category
B3-B evidence) and that new literature is insufficient to further evaluate that finding; and that
assessment of urine output and voiding should be done on a case-by-case basis for selected
patients or selected procedures.

So the honest teaching structure for Day 15 is: the guideline declines to endorse the convention,
a majority of surveyed members actively favour dropping it, and case-by-case assessment for
selected patients is what replaces it. Note the guideline is now thirteen years old and I did not
find a successor; confirm that before the day asserts it is current.

## 5. Day 16, which you expect to change

I agree it is the weakest, and for a specific reason: it is two days wearing one coat. "PACU
capacity is an operating room problem" is an operations claim about flow, staffing and case
scheduling. "Handover is a measurable risk point" is a communication-safety claim. They share a
room and nothing else. The ledger reflects this — I found five sources for the handover half and
zero for the capacity half, because the capacity literature is operations-research work
(Dexter and colleagues on PACU staffing and OR scheduling) that sits outside the quality bar this
ledger was built to, and would need a separate retrieval pass against a different set of journals.

Three candidate replacements, in the order I would rank them:

**16a. The PACU is where the anaesthetic ends and nobody owns the patient.**
Handover as a transfer of *responsibility*, not of information — who is accountable between the
moment the anaesthesiologist leaves and the moment the PACU nurse accepts. This keeps HATRICC,
Salzwedel and the Agarwala consensus recommendations, which are the sources that actually exist,
and drops the capacity material entirely. It also lands closer to what residents get wrong: they
treat handover as a report rather than as a handoff of duty.

**16b. Every PACU discharge decision is a prediction, and the failure mode is asymmetric.**
Pairs naturally with Days 14 and 15, both of which are already about the boundary of the PACU
stay. The evidence base is the same guideline material plus the OSA monitoring literature, so it
needs no new retrieval. It also gives the four-week arc a proper ending: the sixteen days would
close on the decision the resident actually makes rather than on a systems observation they
cannot act on.

**16c. Keep capacity, but make it about the resident's decision, not the institution's.**
"Deciding to keep a patient in the PACU longer is a resource decision made by someone who does
not see the queue." That is defensible and teachable, but I could not source it, and it would
need a retrieval pass into operations-research literature that this ledger explicitly excluded.

My preference is **16a**, on the grounds that it is the only one of the three whose evidence I
have already verified.

## 6. Currency section, mandatory step

Everything found in this category, including items that only slightly qualify a claim.

**Withdrawn drug — edrophonium (Days 1, and any reversal content).** Edrophonium is discontinued
in the United States, under every brand: Enlon, Enlon-Plus, Reversol and Tensilon. The
edrophonium/atropine combination Enlon-Plus is specifically recorded as no longer available in
the US, and all approved formulations are listed as discontinued. Given the failure you describe
in the predecessor curriculum, this is very likely the agent involved. Any page, pearl or quiz
item that offers edrophonium as a reversal option, including as an alternative during a
neostigmine shortage, is now wrong for a US audience. Note that APSF material discussing
edrophonium as a neostigmine-shortage workaround is still online and still reads as current.
*(Verified 2026-09-02 via US drug-availability records; confirm against the FDA discontinued
drug product list before the statement goes into a quiz item.)*

**Superseded guideline — PONV (Day 9).** Gan 2020, the Fourth Consensus Guidelines, is superseded
by the Fifth, now in print at Anesth Analg 2026;143(3):497-513. Detailed in section 3.

**Superseded guideline — postoperative delirium (Day 12).** ESAIC 2017 superseded by ESAIC 2024.

**Superseded assay generation — MINS (Day 8).** Devereaux 2012 used fourth-generation troponin T
with a 0.03 ng/mL threshold. Devereaux 2017 uses fifth-generation hsTnT with a different
threshold structure (20-65 ng/L with absolute change >5 ng/L, or >=65 ng/L, judged ischaemic).
A curriculum quoting 0.03 ng/mL is quoting an assay generation most US labs no longer run. This
is the kind of silent obsolescence that looks fine on the page.

**Erratum affecting a quoted number — Day 4.** Taenzer 2010's rescue-event rates were published
with the wrong denominator label and corrected ten years later (Anesthesiology 2020;132(3):602,
PMID 31868773). The correct denominator is **patient days**, not patient discharges. Many
secondary sources still reproduce the original label. If the day quotes 3.4 to 1.2, it must say
per 1000 patient days.

**Correction notice affecting a reproduced graphic — Day 11.** The ASRA LAST checklist 2020
version carries a correction notice: Figure 1 was replaced after online-first publication. Any
checklist graphic used in teaching material must come from the corrected version or directly from
asra.com, not from an early PDF.

**Regulatory action — Day 12, if dexmedetomidine appears.** The German federal medicines agency
(BfArM) issued a Rote-Hand-Brief in 2022 warning of increased mortality with dexmedetomidine in
intensive care patients aged 65 or younger. This is cited in the German commentary literature
around the 2024 ESAIC guideline. It is a European regulatory action and I did not confirm a
parallel US action; check the current US labelling before any delirium or agitation page
recommends dexmedetomidine.

**Not found in this category:** no retraction affected any source in this ledger. No device
withdrawal affected any source. I did not find a regulatory action bearing on droperidol beyond
the long-standing black box warning, which is discussed but not resolved in the PONV guideline
literature (Ludwin and Shafer's "con" piece is cited in the Fifth guidelines' reference list);
if Day 9 mentions droperidol, read that exchange before writing the rescue page.

## 7. Days with no rows at all

**Day 10 (PACU opioid titration; endpoint is function, not a score) — zero verified sources.**
I did not find a primary source that would survive the quality bar. The titration literature is
largely single-centre protocol descriptions and narrative review, both of which you excluded as
support. The "endpoint is function, not a score" claim is a clinical-reasoning position I could
not attach to a citable trial or guideline statement. Reporting this rather than padding it. If
the day is to be kept, it likely needs either a targeted search on morphine titration protocols
in the PACU (Aubrun and colleagues are the group to look for) or acceptance that it is a
teaching position stated as such, tagged consensus_only on **expert_consensus** with no
supporting numbers.

**Day 13 (structured differential for delayed emergence; neurologic exam before leaving) — zero
verified sources.** Same situation. Delayed emergence is covered in review and textbook material,
both excluded. The "exam must happen before the patient leaves" claim is a reasonable standard
that I could not source to a society statement. Worth one targeted search against ASA and ESAIC
standards documents before deciding whether the day survives.

**Days 6 and 12 and 14 and 15 are thin rather than empty** — two, two, two and one row
respectively, against your three-to-six target. Day 15's single source is sufficient because that
source is the entire argument. Days 6, 12 and 14 are genuinely under-sourced in this pass and
would benefit from more retrieval time rather than from different judgement.
