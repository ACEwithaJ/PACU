## Why it matters

Two things arrive in the PACU under a working regional block, and both are easy to misread as the block. The first is compartment syndrome in a limb that cannot feel it, a question on which the strongest society statement and everyday clinical practice pull in different directions [[ivani-2015]]. The second is local anesthetic systemic toxicity, which the current ASRA advisory describes as presenting in changed patterns, including delayed and atypical presentations, so that it looks unlike the block regression it is mistaken for [[neal-2018]].

The consequence of getting this wrong is a limb, or a patient, whose deterioration is filed under the block.

## Mechanism

A peripheral nerve block interrupts conduction in the nerves that would carry the pain of a rising compartment pressure, which is the whole basis of the worry that it masks compartment syndrome. Against that, ischemic pain is intense and often breaks through a sensory block as the compartment pressure rises, which is the basis of the clinical practice that treats breakthrough pain as the signal. Both mechanisms are real; the disagreement is about which dominates, and the evidence cannot yet say.

Local anesthetic toxicity is a function of plasma concentration at the heart and the brain. After a block, that concentration depends on the dose, the site's vascularity and the rate of systemic absorption, which can be slow. Absorption from a large-volume infiltration or from a depot preparation can produce a peak long after the injection, which is why toxicity can present in the PACU rather than in the block room, and why it can present with cardiovascular rather than neurological signs first [[neal-2018]].

## Evidence

The joint ESRA/ASRA committee practice advisory states that there are no current evidence-based data that the use of regional anesthesia increases the risk of acute compartment syndrome or delays its diagnosis, and that high-level evidence is not available for the topics it evaluated, with most recommendations based on Evidence B studies [[ivani-2015]]. Its scope is pediatric, which is a real limitation for an adult curriculum and is stated here rather than buried.

The 2015 ASRA advisory on neurological complications supplies the framework for distinguishing prolonged block, block failure and neurological injury on examination [[neal-2015]].

The 2018 ASRA interim update on local anesthetic systemic toxicity covers the mechanisms of lipid resuscitation, updated frequency estimates for toxicity, the preventive role of ultrasound guidance, changes in case presentation patterns, and the limited evidence around local infiltration anesthesia and liposomal bupivacaine; it is the source for the atypical and delayed presentations that make toxicity look unlike block regression [[neal-2018]]. The 2021 ASRA checklist is the current cognitive aid, updated in response to user feedback and simulation studies; it carries a correction notice, because its Figure 1 was replaced after online-first publication, so any reproduced checklist must come from the corrected version or from the society's own site [[neal-2021-checklist]].

## What this does not show

The compartment syndrome question is not resolved on this page and the page does not pretend otherwise. The advisory finds no evidence of delayed diagnosis; it does not find evidence against delay, and it says so by recording that high-level evidence is not available [[ivani-2015]]. The opposing position now has a society source. The Association of Anaesthetists guideline on regional analgesia for lower leg trauma advises avoiding neuraxial or peripheral techniques that produce dense blocks of long duration significantly exceeding the duration of surgery; records that single-shot or continuous peripheral blocks with lower concentrations and no adjuncts are not associated with diagnostic delay provided surveillance is appropriate; requires ward observations at set frequencies by staff trained to recognize compartment syndrome, objective scoring charts, and documented consent; and states that neither surgeon nor anesthesiologist may veto the other's recommended treatment [[nathanson-2021]]. The specific wording that breakthrough pain in a blocked limb is the trigger to examine is not in that guideline's abstract: [TODO_VERIFY: the full text of the Association of Anaesthetists guideline for the breakthrough-pain wording.] The two positions are not in fact opposed on the bedside rule; they differ on whether a block delays the diagnosis, and the status chip says uncertain.

Every source here is an advisory or a checklist. None is a trial, and the frequency estimates in the 2018 update have not been extracted, so no incidence figure appears [[neal-2018]].

The lipid emulsion dose is on the corrected ASRA checklist: for a patient over 70 kg, a bolus of about 100 mL over 2 to 3 min then an infusion of about 250 mL over 15 to 20 min; under 70 kg, a bolus of about 1.5 mL/kg over 2 to 3 min then about 0.25 mL/kg/min; if unstable, repeat the bolus and double the infusion; maximum lipid dose 12 mL/kg; epinephrine in smaller than normal doses, starting below 1 µg/kg; avoid local anesthetics, beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers and vasopressin; benzodiazepines preferred for seizures; observe for 2 h after a seizure or 4 to 6 h after cardiovascular instability [[neal-2021-checklist]]. The society states that the checklist is under review with an update expected in early 2027 [[neal-2021-checklist]].

The interscalene block's effect on the diaphragm is not a complication but a consequence: ipsilateral hemidiaphragmatic paresis on ultrasound in 13 of 13 patients within 5 min of a paresthesia-technique block with 34 to 52 mL of 1.5% mepivacaine, resolving by 3 to 5 h [[urmey-1991]]. In a prospective series of 520 interscalene blocks for shoulder surgery, acute complications were one pneumothorax (0.2%) and one episode of central nervous system toxicity (0.2%); symptoms such as paresthesia or non-surgical pain were present in 14% at 10 days, 7.9% at one month, 0.9% at six months and 0.2% at nine months [[borgeat-2001]]. Horner syndrome is not reported in that abstract: [NUMBER NEEDED: incidence of Horner syndrome after interscalene block]. Pneumothorax after ultrasound-guided supraclavicular or infraclavicular block occurred in 4 of 6366 blocks (0.06%, 95% CI 0.001 to 0.124), three of the four presenting after a two-day latency, against a historical non-ultrasound risk quoted as up to 6.1% [[gauss-2014]]. The latency is the PACU point: the pneumothorax that follows a supraclavicular block is usually not in the PACU yet.

## At the bedside

Pain that escalates through a block that was working is a finding to explain, not a top-up to give. Examine the limb, measure what your institution measures, and involve the surgeon early; the advisory's absence of evidence for delay is not a reason to wait [[ivani-2015]]. [PRACTICE VARIES: whether a block is used at all in limbs at risk of compartment syndrome, and who is called when breakthrough pain occurs, differ between institutions and surgical services.]

Know the current ASRA checklist and where the corrected version lives, because toxicity in the PACU may present hours after the block and with the heart rather than the brain [[neal-2021-checklist]] [[neal-2018]]. Have lipid emulsion where you can reach it and know the local dose.

When a block seems prolonged or a new deficit appears, use the advisory's framework to separate prolonged block from failure from injury before you reassure anyone [[neal-2015]].
